I am an experimental physicist specializing in atomic, molecular, and optical (AMO) physics. My research connects quantum computing and precision measurement through a common question: how precisely can we control the quantum states of individual atoms, and how can we use that control to deepen our understanding of physics? My work spans two closely connected directions: engineering cold-atom systems for quantum computation and building optical lattice clocks to probe fundamental physics.

I am currently a postdoctoral researcher at UC Berkeley working with Shimon Kolkowitz. We are building an optical lattice clock to probe gravitational redshift by comparing atoms at different heights. I also explore how ideas from quantum computing and quantum simulation can improve precision measurement, including using erasure conversion to extend clock coherence and Aubry–André localization to suppress atomic tunneling.

During my Ph.D. at Princeton with Jeff Thompson, I helped establish one of the first tweezer-array platforms for quantum computing based on ytterbium-171. My work included developing experimental tools to encode and control nuclear-spin qubits, perform high-fidelity quantum gates, and detect errors during a computation. I began my research as an undergraduate in David Weld’s group at UC Santa Barbara.
